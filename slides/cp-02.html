Learn

The standards, the limits of confidentiality, and the boundaries that protect clients and you.

Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries5

The standards

NOHS Ethical Standards

The National Organization for Human Services publishes the ethical standards for our field. They are organized by who you are responsible to:

Clients

Respect, dignity, informed consent, privacy, and acting in their best interest

The public and society

Advocacy, fairness, and respect for diverse communities

Colleagues

Cooperation, respect, and addressing ethical concerns properly

Employers

Following agency policy and raising concerns through the right channels

The profession

Honesty about your skills and credentials, and continued learning

Self

Self-awareness, self-care, and knowing your own biases
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries6
Hand out or project the current NOHS Ethical Standards (available on the NOHS website). Do not ask learners to memorize standard numbers. The skill is knowing which area applies and what it asks of you. The HS-BCP exam expects learners to apply ethics to scenarios, so connect every example today back to one of these areas.

Confidentiality

What stays private, and why

  • Confidentiality means what a client tells you stays inside the agency and is shared only with the people who need it to help them.
  • Informed consent means the client knows what you'll do, what you'll share, with whom, and why, and agrees to it.
  • A release of information is the signed form that lets you share specific information with a specific agency.

Never without consent

Whether someone is your client at all. Their health, mental health, or substance use history. Their immigration status, criminal record, or address. What they told you in private.
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries7
Stress that even confirming someone is a client breaks confidentiality. Practice the phrase: “I'm not able to say whether someone receives services here.” Ask learners to repeat it out loud. Mention that some records have extra federal protection, such as substance use treatment records (42 CFR Part 2) and health information (HIPAA). We go deeper on records in Module 7.

The limits

When you can't keep it private

Tell clients the limits at the start, before they share, not after.

SituationWhat it means for you
Risk of serious harm to selfFollow agency safety procedures and get your supervisor right away
Threat of serious harm to someone elseFollow agency procedures. You may need to warn or report.
Suspected child abuse or neglectMandated report (next slide)
Suspected abuse of a vulnerable adultReport under agency policy and DC law
Court orderYour supervisor and agency legal staff handle the response
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries8
Model how to explain the limits in plain words: “What you tell me stays private, with a few exceptions. If I'm worried you might be hurt or might hurt someone, or if a child is being hurt, I have to get help. I'll always tell you if that happens.” Have learners practice saying it to a partner in their own words.

Mandated reporting · DC

If you suspect a child is being hurt

  1. You don't need proof. Reasonable suspicion is enough.
  2. Don't investigate or question the child in detail.
  3. Call the DC Child and Family Services Agency (CFSA) hotline: (202) 671-SAFE (7233), 24 hours.
  4. Tell your supervisor and follow agency policy.
  5. Write down what you saw and heard, in facts, the same day.

Remember

The report is yours to make. Telling your supervisor doesn't replace it unless your agency's policy says the supervisor files it with you.
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries9
Confirm before class: the CFSA hotline number, and the current list of mandated reporters in the DC Code (social service workers are on it). Some learners may have had CFSA involvement in their own families. Acknowledge that this can bring up strong feelings. The purpose of a report is safety and support for the family, and the practitioner's job is to report, not to decide.

Boundaries

Working in your own community

A dual relationship is when you have another connection to a client besides being their practitioner: neighbor, church member, relative, old friend, landlord, coworker.

In a close community, some overlap can't be avoided. The goal is to manage it, not pretend it isn't there.

Manage it

Tell your supervisor. Ask if the case should go to a coworker. Talk with the client about how you'll act if you see each other outside. Never use what you learned at work in your personal life.
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries10
This is where lived experience is both a strength and a risk. Ask: “Who here would almost certainly run into a client at the store, at church, or on the bus?” Most hands go up. Normalize it. Then ask: “What would you say if a client waved and said, ‘Hey, you're my caseworker!’ in front of your friends?” Practice: let the client lead. If they greet you, greet them back. Never mention the agency.

Gray areas

Gifts, social media, and sharing your story

SituationUsually okayUsually not okay
GiftsA thank-you card, a small token, under agency policyMoney, services, expensive gifts, anything that creates a debt
Social mediaAn agency page with agency rulesFriending or following clients, posting about your day at work
Self-disclosureA brief share, only when it helps the clientLong stories, sharing to feel better yourself, sharing details you haven't healed from

Always check your agency's written policy. When in doubt, ask your supervisor first.

Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries11
Self-disclosure is a big topic for a lived experience course. Teach the test: “Who is this for?” If it is for the client, keep it short and bring it back to them. If it is for you, save it for supervision or your own support. Peer roles may share more by design. Practitioner roles share less.

A method

Six steps for an ethical decision

  1. Name the problem. What is the conflict, and whose interests are involved?
  2. Check the standards and the rules. NOHS standards, agency policy, and the law.
  3. Think about who is affected. The client, family, community, agency, and you.
  4. List the options and what could happen with each.
  5. Consult. Talk with your supervisor before you act, unless someone is in danger right now.
  6. Act and document. Do it, then write down what you did and why.
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries12
Teams will use these six steps in Practice. Point out that step 5 is not optional for a new practitioner. Asking is a strength, not a weakness. Supervisors would always rather hear about a dilemma before than after.

Quick check 10 min

Okay, not okay, or it depends?

  1. A client's mother calls and asks if her son came to his appointment.
  2. A client gives you a homemade card that says thank you.
  3. You see a client at the grocery store. They don't say hello, so you don't either.
  4. A 10-year-old tells you her dad hit her with a belt last night.
  5. You share a client's story with your best friend, without a name.
Module 02 · Ethics & Boundaries13
Answers: 1) Not okay to confirm without a release. 2) Usually okay, check policy. 3) Okay, let the client lead. 4) Mandated report. 5) Not okay. Details can identify someone, especially in a small community. Move quickly; Practice goes deeper.

The case · fictional

Meet Tanya

Tanya, 29, came to a neighborhood resource center for help with two months of back rent. She has a 6-year-old son.

  • She lives three blocks from the center
  • One outreach worker knows her family from church
  • Several staff each hit an ethical question this week
  • Today's team meeting is an ethics case conference

What Tanya says

“I almost didn't come in. Everybody around here knows everybody. I need to know my business stays my business.”

Read your card

  • Read the facts only you know

Walk the room. Some learners will want to jump to an answer. Ask them to name the question first. For the family support worker, quietly confirm they understand this is a mandated report.

Step 2 · team huddle 40 min

Run the case conference

For each role's question, the Team lead walks through the six steps:

  1. Name the problem
  2. Check the standards and rules
  3. Who is affected?
  4. List the options
  5. Consult
  6. Act and document

Decision table

Write each situation, your call (okay, not okay, or it depends), the standard, and what you'll do.

Teams often spend too long on the first question. Call time every 8 minutes. Make sure every team handles the child's bruise as a required report and decides who calls and when.

What will you say to Tanya?
