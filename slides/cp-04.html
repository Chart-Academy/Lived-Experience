Opening circle 15 min

The best listener you know

Think of someone who really listens to you. When you talk with them, what do they do, with their face, their body, their words, that tells you they're listening?

Share as much or as little as you want. You can always pass.

Module 04 · Communication Skills4
Write the answers on the board in two columns: what they do with their body, and what they say. Body: eye contact, nodding, putting the phone down, leaning in. Words: asking more, saying back what you said, not jumping in with advice. You've just built the active listening checklist together.

Part 1

Learn

The skills that make people want to keep talking.

Module 04 · Communication Skills5

Active listening

Listening is something you do

  • Attend: face the person, put your phone away, use a calm and open posture
  • Follow: short encouragers like “mm-hm,” “go on,” and silence
  • Reflect: say back the meaning or the feeling
  • Summarize: pull it together and check that you got it right

Silence is a skill

Wait three to five seconds after a person stops talking. Many people say the most important thing after a pause.
Module 04 · Communication Skills6
Try it: ask a volunteer a simple question and count five seconds of silence out loud after they answer. Ask the class how it felt. Most people rush to fill silence. Clients notice.

Questions

Open and closed questions

Closed (yes, no, or a fact)Open (tell me more)
“Do you have a job?”“Tell me about your work over the last year.”
“Are you okay with that plan?”“What do you think about that plan?”
“Did you go to the appointment?”“How did the appointment go?”
“Why didn't you call?”“What got in the way of calling?”

Closed questions are useful for facts, like a date of birth. Open questions are how you learn what matters.

Module 04 · Communication Skills7
Point out the last row: “why” questions can sound like blame, even when you don't mean it. “What got in the way” invites the story without judgment.

Motivational interviewing

OARS

O · Open questions

“What would make the biggest difference for you right now?”

A · Affirmations

“You kept going to that job even when the bus was two hours each way. That takes commitment.”

R · Reflections

“You're tired of starting over, and you want something that lasts.”

S · Summaries

“So far you've said housing is first, then the job. Did I get that right?”
Module 04 · Communication Skills8
Motivational interviewing (MI), developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, is a way of talking with people about change that respects their own reasons. Practitioners use the basic skills. Full MI takes more training. Affirmations must be specific and true, not empty praise.

Motivational interviewing

Listen for change talk

When people talk about change, they often feel two ways. MI helps them hear their own reasons.

  • Desire: “I want…”
  • Ability: “I could…”
  • Reasons: “It would help my kids if…”
  • Need: “I have to…”

The spirit of MI

Partnership, acceptance, compassion, and drawing out the person's own ideas. Not pushing, lecturing, or arguing.
Module 04 · Communication Skills9
When you hear change talk, reflect it back: “You want your kids to see you working.” Avoid the “righting reflex,” the urge to fix the problem for the client. Telling people what to do usually makes them argue for not changing.

Meet people where they are

Adapting how you communicate

If the person…Try…
Speaks another languageA trained interpreter or language line, never their child. Speak to the client, not the interpreter.
Has limited readingPlain words, read forms aloud, “teach-back”: “Tell me in your words what we'll do next.”
Is hard of hearingFace them, quiet room, don't shout, write key points, ask what works best
Comes from a different cultureAsk, don't assume. “What's important for me to understand about you and your family?”
Module 04 · Communication Skills10
Teach-back is one of the most useful tools in the course. It checks your explanation, not the client's intelligence. Say: “I want to make sure I explained it well.” Under federal civil rights law, agencies that receive federal funds must provide language access. Using a child to interpret puts the child in an adult role.

De-escalation

When someone is upset

  1. Safety first. Stay between the person and nothing; keep your own exit clear.
  2. Lower your voice and slow down.
  3. Name the feeling: “You're really frustrated. That makes sense.”
  4. Don't argue the facts yet.
  5. Offer a choice: “Do you want to sit down, or take a minute?”
  6. Get help if anyone is in danger. Follow your agency's safety plan.

Remember

Anger is often fear or helplessness. The person is angry at the situation, even if it's aimed at you.
Module 04 · Communication Skills11
Connect to Module 3: anger can be a fight response. Practice the line “You're really frustrated, and you have every reason to be” with the whole room. Make sure learners know never to stay in an unsafe situation to finish a conversation.

Quick check 10 min

Make it open

  1. “Do you have kids?”
  2. “Are you looking for work?”
  3. “Did the shelter help you?”
  4. “Why did you miss your appointment?”
  5. “Is everything okay at home?”
Module 04 · Communication Skills12
Have learners rewrite each one out loud. Sample answers: “Tell me about your family.” “What kind of work are you thinking about?” “What was the shelter like for you?” “What got in the way of the appointment?” “How are things at home?”

Part 2

Practice

Practice in triads. Every person plays practitioner, client, and observer.

Module 04 · Communication Skills13
Split each team of six into two triads. Each triad runs three rounds, and the roles rotate every round. Teams of 4 or 5: one triad, and the extra people observe.

The case · fictional

Three clients

Three clients are waiting at a neighborhood service center today.

  • Mr. Curtis Bell, 71: veteran, hard of hearing
  • Ms. Ana Morales, 44: more comfortable in Spanish
  • Mr. Darnell Price, 35: angry about a benefits cut
  • Each one has something important they won't say unless you listen well

What one client says

“Nobody ever asks me what I think. They just hand me a paper and tell me where to sign.”
Module 04 · Communication Skills14
Explain that the client cards have hidden facts. If the practitioner asks a closed question, the client gives a short answer. If they ask open questions and reflect, the client shares more. Clients: play it honestly. Don't make it impossible.
