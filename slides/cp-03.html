Part 1

Learn

What trauma does, what helps, and how to protect yourself in this work.

Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice5

The basics

What trauma is

SAMHSA describes trauma with three Es:

  • Event: what happened, once, many times, or over years
  • Experience: how the person lived it. The same event affects people differently.
  • Effects: lasting effects on health, trust, relationships, and daily life

It's common

Many people who come for services have lived through violence, loss, incarceration, homelessness, racism, or community violence. Assume anyone could have a trauma history, without asking them to prove it.
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice6
This framework comes from SAMHSA's Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach (2014). Name historical and community trauma, not just individual events. Keep the focus on the practitioner's role: we don't diagnose or treat trauma. We make our services safer.

The four Rs

A trauma-informed program…

Realizes

how common trauma is and how it affects people

Recognizes

the signs of trauma in clients, families, and staff

Responds

by building this knowledge into policies, practices, and language

Resists re-traumatization

by changing what hurts people, even by accident
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice7
Ask: “Have you ever been in a service setting that re-traumatized you or someone you know?” Examples learners often name: being searched, waiting for hours, telling their story over and over, being talked about in front of them, rules enforced with no explanation.

Trauma responses

When the body feels danger

ResponseWhat it can look like in an officeWhat can help
FightRaised voice, arguing, “attitude”Stay calm, lower your voice, give space and choices
FlightLeaving, missing appointments, rushingShorter visits, clear next steps, follow up
FreezeGoing quiet, blank stare, “I don't know”Slow down, check in, offer a break
FawnAgreeing to everything, over-apologizingCheck real understanding, make it safe to say no
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice8
These are survival responses, not character flaws. A client who “has an attitude” may be in fight mode. A client who “isn't motivated” may be in freeze. Ask: “How would you have described this client before today? How would you describe them now?”

SAMHSA's six principles

A trauma-informed approach

1. Safety

Physical and emotional safety for clients and staff

2. Trustworthiness and transparency

Clear explanations, decisions made openly

3. Peer support

People with lived experience help build safety and hope

4. Collaboration and mutuality

Sharing power. Healing happens in relationships.

5. Empowerment, voice, and choice

Building on strengths and real choices

6. Cultural, historical, and gender issues

Recognizing bias, history, and identity

Go back to the opening list on the board. Match each item to a principle. Point out principle 3: peer support is a core part of a trauma-informed approach. That is where many people in this room fit.

Taking care of you

Burnout and vicarious trauma

  • Vicarious trauma: taking on the effects of trauma by hearing others' stories again and again
  • Compassion fatigue: feeling numb, drained, or unable to care as much
  • Burnout: exhaustion from workload and stress over time

Warning signs

Trouble sleeping, irritability, dreading work, feeling hopeless, thinking about clients' stories at home, using more alcohol or other substances. Lived experience can make this hit closer to home.
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice11
Say clearly: having your own history does not make you a worse practitioner. It means you need a plan. Your plan should include supervision, someone outside work you can talk to, and a way to leave work at work. Remind learners of 988 and the employee assistance programs many agencies offer.

Quick check 10 min

Which principle?

  1. Telling a client exactly how long the intake will take and what happens next.
  2. Letting a client choose whether to meet in the office or by phone.
  3. A peer specialist who has been homeless co-leads the housing group.
  4. Making sure the waiting room has no TV playing violent news.
  5. Offering forms in the client's first language.
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice12
Answers: 1) Trustworthiness and transparency. 2) Empowerment, voice, and choice. 3) Peer support. 4) Safety. 5) Cultural, historical, and gender issues. More than one principle can fit. Accept good reasoning.

Part 2

Practice

Step into a role and redesign an intake that hurt the person it was meant to help.

Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice13
Hand out the in-class packet. Remind learners that Rosa is fictional. If the case brings up something personal for anyone, they can step out or switch to the Team lead role.

The case · fictional

Meet Rosa

Rosa, 37, left an abusive partner three weeks ago and is staying with a friend with her 12-year-old son.

  • She came to a family services center for help
  • The intake took place in a busy room
  • Halfway through the form, she got up and left
  • Your team's job: fix the intake so the next Rosa stays

What Rosa told a friend

“They asked me why I stayed so long. I didn't have an answer that wouldn't make me cry in front of a stranger. So I left.”

Read the case slowly. Pause after the quote. Ask: “Which principle did that question break?” (Safety; voice and choice.) Then hand out the cards.

Step 1 · on your own, in role 20 min

Read your card and the old form

  • Read the facts only you know
  • Read the eight old intake questions on the team worksheet
  • Mark the ones your role thinks caused harm
  • Which of SAMHSA's six principles do they break?
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice16
Most learners will mark almost every question. That's fine. Ask them to pick the two worst from their role's point of view.

Step 2 · team huddle 50 min

Rewrite the intake

  1. Each role shares what they know
  2. For each old question: keep it, change it, move it later, or drop it?
  3. Write the rewrite and the principle

Test every question

Do we need this today? Does Rosa know why we're asking? Can she skip it? Is it in plain words?
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice17
Push teams to drop or delay questions, not just reword them. A trauma-informed intake asks only what is needed now. Check that teams handle the address question safely.

Step 3 · plan and present 40 min

The new first five minutes

  • What staff say first when Rosa sits down
  • Three changes to the room or process
  • Your team's best rewrite and its principle
Present in 2 minutes. Each person says one sentence in role.
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice18
Ask one team to act out their first five minutes, with the Intake worker greeting the instructor as Rosa.

Debrief · whole class 30 min

Step out of role

  1. Which old question surprised you most once you saw it through Rosa's eyes?
  2. What changes cost nothing at all?
  3. Where have you seen services do this well?
  4. How are you feeling after this case? What do you need right now?
Module 03 · Trauma-Informed Practice19
End the debrief with a grounding moment: one slow breath together, or name one thing you can see, hear, and feel. Check in with anyone who seemed affected before the break.
