Language

Facts and strengths, not labels

Instead of…Write…
“Dramatic, crying again.”“Participant cried while talking about custody. Took a break and returned.”
“Non-compliant, missed sessions.”“Missed 10/2 and 10/9. Said child care fell through both days.”
“Still with her abuser.”Leave it out unless needed for safety planning, and then only the minimum.
“Ex-offender” / “prostitute”“Returning citizen” / “survivor of trafficking,” only if relevant and allowed
Module 08 · Documentation & Referral7
Labels follow women into court and custody cases. Ask: “Would she feel respected reading this?”

Privacy and safety

Records can put her in danger

  • Never write a shelter address or her new address in shared notes
  • Minimum necessary: only what's needed for the service
  • Releases: share only what she has agreed to, with whom she has agreed
  • Records can be subpoenaed in court and custody cases
  • Lock paper files. Use secure systems, not personal email or texts.

Before you write

“If her abuser's lawyer read this, could it hurt her?”
Module 08 · Documentation & Referral8
Survivor programs often have special confidentiality rules, including under federal funding laws for domestic violence services. Learners should ask about their agency's rules. Confirm current requirements before class.

Referrals

Referrals that connect

  1. Check it: the resource is current and she's eligible
  2. Ask her: does she want it? What worries her?
  3. Get consent to share, in writing
  4. Warm handoff: call together, introduce her
  5. Log it and set a follow-up date
  6. Follow up: did she connect? How did it go?

For survivors

Check that the program is safe for her: women-only if she prefers, and no chance of running into her abuser.

Proofread

Four checks before you save

  1. Accurate: dates, names, quotes
  2. Objective: facts and strengths, no labels
  3. Safe and private: no location, minimum necessary
  4. Professional: clear, no slang or texting shortcuts
Never change a saved note. Add a dated correction, following agency policy.

The case · fictional

Meet Yvonne

Yvonne, 42, is a survivor of labor trafficking. She has been in a women's workforce program for two months.

  • Lives in confidential housing for survivors
  • Wants to work as a home health aide
  • Five staff wrote notes or referrals this week
  • Every one has problems

What Yvonne says

“The people who held me wrote everything down about me. I need to know what you're writing, and who sees it.”
Module 08 · Documentation & Referral14
For a trafficking survivor, records can feel like control. Yvonne's request is reasonable: she can see her records and know who they're shared with.

Step 1 · on your own, in role 20 min

Find the problems

  • Read your weak note or referral
  • Read what really happened
  • List every problem: labels, guesses, location, over-sharing, missing next steps
  • Write your own rewrite first
Module 08 · Documentation & Referral16
Learners will spot the labels quickly. Push them to find the safety problems.

Step 2 · team huddle 50 min

Check and fix

  1. Each role reads its note out loud
  2. Run the four checks
  3. Rewrite it together
  4. Ask: “Would Yvonne be okay reading this?”

Safety first

Which note could put Yvonne in danger if the wrong person saw it?
Module 08 · Documentation & Referral17
The housing note includes her confidential address. The referral email shares her trafficking history and immigration status without consent. Both are serious.

Step 3 · plan and present 40 min

One strong note, one safe referral, one log

  • Rewrite the referral email within what Yvonne agreed to share
  • Build the referral log for this week
Present in 2 minutes. Read your referral email out loud.
